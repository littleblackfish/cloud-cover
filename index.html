Notes for a new observer

Worked out from geometry. The rules section summarises common practice; the authority for Canadian observing is ECCC's MANOBS and your instructor, so check anything that matters there.

1. What an octa measures

Cloud amount is the fraction of the celestial dome hidden by cloud. The dome is the whole half of the sky above your horizon, every direction you could look from where you stand. An octa is one eighth of it. What counts is how much of your view is covered (its solid angle). It is not the ground area under the cloud, and it is not the area on a photograph.

2. The dome's area is not where you expect it

The sky area above elevation e is proportional to 1 − sin e. So the fraction of the sky below e is sin e. Some results:

  • Half the dome lies below 30°. (sin 30° = ½)
  • The lowest octa is the strip from the horizon up to only 7°.
  • The octa around the zenith is a circle reaching down to 61°, only 29° from straight up.
Octas of sky belowElevation

Why: imagine the dome as a stack of rings. A ring at elevation e has circumference proportional to cos e. It is long near the horizon and shrinks to a point overhead. Low rings hold most of the area.

3. Slices and quadrants

Cut the dome like an orange, from the horizon to the zenith. Every 45° wedge is exactly one octa, and every quadrant (N‑E, E‑S…) is two. A common way to estimate: judge each quadrant in eighths, then add them up. A slice looks wide at the horizon and comes to a point overhead, but each slice has the same area.

You can also lay the orange on its side, with its axis along the horizon from north to south. Now each slice is bounded by arcs that run 180° from the north horizon, over the sky, to the south horizon. The dome still splits into eight equal slices, four on each side of the zenith. The slices next to the horizon look thin, and the ones overhead look wide, but every one is an octa. Try Vert slice with the 8 vert slices guides.

4. Your eye squashes the dome

Most people see the sky as a flattened bowl, not a hemisphere. Asked to point halfway between horizon and zenith, they usually point around 20–30°, not 45°. So check elevations with your hand. At arm's length a closed fist is about 10° and a spread hand about 20°. Three fists up from the horizon is roughly 30°, and all the sky below that line is half the dome.

5. The packing effect: a layer looks thicker near the horizon

A cloud overhead shows you its flat base. A cloud far off near the horizon shows you its side. With cumulus that have real depth, the sides of distant clouds fill the gaps between them. A layer with 3/8 real coverage can look solid low down. The Cloud layer tab shows this. Make the cloud thickness small to remove the effect, or large (towering cumulus) to make it strong. High clouds are thin, so they show it least.

Distance also matters. A ray at elevation e meets a cloud base at height h about h / tan e away. For a 4000 ft (1200 m) base, the top half of your sky (above 30°) looks at a circle of cloud only about 2 km in radius. The bottom half looks at a ring stretching out to about 120 km, where the Earth's curvature brings the layer down to your horizon. That is thousands of times more cloud area. Low sky is where distant weather and other layers pile up.

Compare that with the ground. With your eye 1.7 m up, the ground horizon is only √(2Rh) ≈ 4.65 km away (R is the Earth's radius, 6371 km). A cloud layer at 4,000 ft only meets your horizon about 125 km away. So the clouds near your horizon are far beyond the ground you can see.

6. Reporting rules (verify against MANOBS)

  • 0 means no cloud at all. Any trace of cloud, even less than an eighth, is reported as at least 1.
  • 8 means fully covered, no gaps. Any blue showing makes it at most 7.
  • Otherwise round to the nearest eighth.
  • METAR terms: FEW 1–2, SCT 3–4, BKN 5–7, OVC 8 octas. The lowest BKN or OVC layer (or a vertical visibility) is the ceiling.
  • Summation principle, used in Canada and the US: the amount given for a layer is the sky hidden by that layer plus all layers below it. Amounts can only grow as you go up. Example: 3/8 Sc at 3000 ft with 2/8 more Ac visible at 10 000 ft becomes SCT030 BKN100.
  • Canadian METARs also carry a remarks group of cloud types with per-layer amounts in octas (e.g. SC3AC2). A thin veil can cover much of the sky while hiding little of it. Ask how MANOBS wants amount and opacity handled.
  • When fog, snow, etc. at the surface hide the sky, the sky is obscured and a vertical visibility is reported instead of layers.

7. Field habits

  • Observe from a spot with as much open horizon as possible. Turn all the way around, and look straight up too.
  • Estimate quadrant by quadrant, then check the total against your first impression.
  • Treat the low sky with suspicion: packing, haze and distant layers all make it look more covered.
  • At night, let your eyes adapt for several minutes. Use stars (where they're missing) and lit cloud bases near towns to find the cloud edges.
  • Never look at the sun. Block it with a building or your hand.

About this tool

Coverage is computed on a 0.5° grid with true solid-angle weights, so the numbers are exact to about 0.1%. In the Octas tab, every deformation keeps the covered area fixed: the shape's outline is the contour that encloses exactly the chosen area. The cloud-layer model uses flat-topped cylinders on a jittered lattice, which is simpler than real clouds but enough to show the geometry.

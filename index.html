The layer is a flat plane parallel to the ground, and the cloud is one flat round patch on it, placed in your sky by azimuth and elevation (past 90° it crosses the zenith). Lower it and the patch becomes a long oval far off. The patch is sized so it covers exactly the chosen octas of the dome, at any height, so the same octas take a bigger cloud the higher the layer: the readout shows its real size on the layer. A cloud type's pattern lies on the layer itself, so its elements crowd together toward the horizon, and the same cloud looks smaller the higher it is.

One cloud layer, parallel to the ground, seen by the observer at the centre. Everything is to scale: the observer's eye is 1.7 m up, and the dome stands on their horizon, 4.65 km away, so the ground inside it is all the ground they can see. Clouds sit at their real heights: low ones inside the dome, higher ones above it. They are drawn out to where the observer's line of sight, grazing the horizon, still reaches them: about 130 km for a 4,000 ft layer. Everything follows the Earth's curve. Beyond the horizon the rest of the planet is drawn faintly. Clouds more than 66 km away sit within about a degree of the horizon, so they are drawn only as a trace (they still count toward the octas). Each étage has its own shade: low clouds greyest, high clouds nearly white. The view pulls back as the layer rises.

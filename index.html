Azimuth is the horizon the cloud starts from; elevation is how far it has climbed from there, over the zenith at 90° and down the far side. Form breaks the shape into cumuli (left) or strati (right). The covered area stays exactly as chosen.

Heights in

The layer is a flat plane parallel to the ground (shown faintly), and the cloud is one flat round patch on it, placed in your sky by azimuth and elevation (past 90° it crosses the zenith). Lower it and the patch becomes a long oval far off. The patch is sized so it covers exactly the chosen octas of the dome, at any height.

One cloud layer, parallel to the ground, seen by the observer at the centre. Everything is to scale: the observer's eye is 1.7 m up, and the dome stands on their horizon, 4.65 km away, so the ground inside it is all the ground they can see. Clouds sit at their real heights: low ones inside the dome, higher ones above it. They are drawn out to where the observer's line of sight, grazing the horizon, still reaches them: about 130 km for a 4,000 ft layer. Everything follows the Earth's curve. Each étage has its own shade: low clouds greyest, high clouds nearly white. The view pulls back as the layer rises.
